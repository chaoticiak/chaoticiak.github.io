Togetrick, invented by me (chaotic_iak / Ivan Koswara), is a campaign-based cooperative trick-taking game for 3–4 players (with a solo variant). It is designed to be playable with a standard deck of cards plus some common components.

This game is still a work in progress. Nothing on this page or its related pages is set in stone.

Overview

Togetrick is a trick-taking game. It is a class of games often found in traditional card games: each player plays one card from their hand, following suit if possible, and the player who played the highest card "wins" the trick. However, unlike traditional games such as contract bridge and Hearts, Togetrick is a cooperative game. You win or lose together as a group, and you need to help each other if you want to succeed.

Togetrick is presented as a campaign, divided into numerous worlds. Worlds have tasks that you can complete by playing rounds of trick-taking gameplay. Completing objectives in worlds gives rewards, including access to other worlds.

The main mechanism in Togetrick, which distinguishes it from other trick-taking games, is the "pool": an additional communal hand which all players may play from, following certain rules. Thus, you don't always play from your hand; you will sometimes play from the pool as well.

Togetrick is also designed to be played with components that are easy to come by: a standard deck of cards, plus several tokens of any kind. Together with the campaign page (and a way to record your progress), you have everything you need to play.

The idea of "campaign-based cooperative trick-taking game" was originally inspired by The Crew series, with some influence from The Lords of the Rings: Trick-Taking Game series. However, Togetrick has since diverged from The Crew enough that it is essentially its own game.

Starting your campaign

If you're new to Togetrick, start by reading this section.

If you're playing solo, you need to also read about the solo variant.

Togetrick is presented as a campaign, divided into numerous worlds. Worlds have tasks that you can complete by playing rounds of trick-taking gameplay, called deals.

At the beginning, you only have access to the world Startia. Open that page. You'll need to reference it, and the campaign page in general, throughout your campaign.

Startia is a regular world, which means it comprises of several tasks (in this case 3), and you will attempt them one by one. Start with task A: "Every player must win at least 1 trick". You need to play a deal to succeed on this task.

To play a deal, first you need to prepare the deal. Follow the instructions based on your player count.

Each player will then need to seed the pool by putting two cards into the pool. The pool is the main mechanism in Togetrick. You may also want to read about playing out tricks to learn how the pool is used.

It's okay if you don't know what two cards to seed into the pool. For now, simply seed any two; the very first task is designed to be winnable with virtually any seeding. Besides, it takes practice and experience to learn how to seed the pool effectively, and experience starts with a first step.

Once the pool has been seeded, you will now play out 8 tricks. Follow the instructions on how to play a trick based on your player count, then repeat until everyone is out of cards. (The pool will have one card remaining.)

The deal is now over. Did you succeed on the task?

If you failed, that's okay. You've learned a lot by playing your first deal. Simply try again, this time with a much better idea of how the game will work.

Open after you succeed on task A

Congratulations for succeeding on your first task! Move on to task B, then task C. Look at the campaign page for the descriptions of these tasks.

Note that task B cares about what happens during the deal, so you need to be aware of it throughout the deal.

If you fail on a task, just retry the task. You don't have to start over from the first task again.

Once you have succeeded on all 3 tasks, you have completed the objective in Startia! The entry for Startia on the campaign page has a block that you can open and read.

Components

The following components are used in all Togetrick games. Some paths require additional components.

  • A deck of 33 cards; see below
  • (3p) 9 / (4p) 8 pool tokens (small objects, such as coins or dice)
  • A captain figure (something with table presence, such as an action figure or the deckbox)

The deck

The 33-card deck is obtained from a standard 52-card deck. Keep the following cards:

  • In ♣ clubs, ♦ diamonds, and ♥ hearts: Aces and 2–10
  • In ♠ spades: Jack, Queen, King

The basic suits are ♣ clubs, ♦ diamonds, and ♥ hearts. Each basic suit has 10 cards, ranked 1 (lowest) to 10 (highest). (In Togetrick, always treat Aces as rank 1. They are the lowest rank.)

The trump suit is ♠ spades. It has 3 cards, ranked Jack (lowest), Queen, King (highest).

A "number rank" means a rank in one of the basic suits. The trump ranks are not number ranks.

Although the remaining cards are not part of the deck, keep them handy. The campaign might make use of them.

Playing a deal

Objectives will require you to play one or more deals. Each deal follows this basic structure unless otherwise instructed. Each deal consists of three phases: preparing the deal, then seeding the pool, and finally playing out tricks.

Preparing the deal

  1. Choose one player to be the captain. They take the captain figure.
  2. Each player takes (3p) 3 pool tokens / (4p) 2 pool tokens.
  3. Shuffle all 33 cards.
  4. Prepare an area of the table to become the pool. Put (3p) 3 cards / (4p) 1 card face-up into the pool.
  5. Distribute the remaining cards to the players, so each player gets (3p) 10 cards / (4p) 8 cards to form their hand.
  6. You might have some instructions to do "after distributing cards". Do them now.

Your hand is private information. You may look at it, but do not show it to any other player or talk about anything in your hand.

Seeding the pool

Before play properly begins, you need to make a major decision: to seed the pool.

  1. Each player simultaneously chooses 2 cards from their hand and puts them face-down on the table.
  2. Once everyone is ready, each player reveals their chosen cards face-up, then places them into the pool.
  3. Organize the pool by suit.

There are 9 cards in the pool, and each player has (3p) 8 cards / (4p) 6 cards left in their hand.

Seeding the pool serves multiple purposes. Most obviously, it lets you adjust your hand. It is also possible to use it to communicate certain key facts to other players, although doing this is far more difficult and requires practice and experience.

You may look at who seeds what cards. You may also ask for a reminder of who seeded a particular card in the pool.

Playing out a trick

Each deal consists of 8 tricks. In each trick, the lead player will play a card, then in clockwise order, each other player will play a card. In the 1st trick of a deal, the lead player is the captain. Afterward, the lead player is the player who won the previous trick.

To play a card, place it face-up in front of you. Typically you will play from your hand; however, in each trick, exactly one player will play from the pool. The main difference between 3p and 4p is whether you play from the pool in addition to or instead of your hand. Read about the pool for more information about how to play from the pool.

Regardless of player count, you are playing a card from somewhere: your hand or the pool. This is called your "set". Except for the lead card (i.e. the first card played in the trick), when you play a card from a set, you must follow suit if able. If the set contains any card matching the suit of the lead card, you must play one of them. If not, you may play any card from the set.

Once all players have had a turn, the trick is over. Regardless of player count, there will be 4 cards in the trick. The winning card is determined as follows:

  1. If the trick contains any trump (♠ spades), the highest trump card wins the trick.
  2. Otherwise, among the cards of the lead suit, the highest card wins the trick. (Note that Aces are always rank 1 and thus are the lowest rank.)

The player who played the winning card gathers the played cards face-up in front of themself. They become the lead player for the next trick.

A deal ends after 8 tricks. All players are out of cards, and the pool has one card remaining. The campaign will tell you what to do with the result of the deal.

When playing a card, the only requirement is that you must follow suit if able. There are no other requirements. In some traditional trick-taking games, you might find rules such as, you can only lead trumps if someone has played it before ("breaking trumps"), or that you must play a higher card if possible, etc. Those rules do not apply in Togetrick.

In traditional trick-taking games, once a trick is won, its cards are turned face-down and nobody may inspect them any more. I personally don't like the memory aspect, so the default rules is that won tricks are left face-up. However, you may choose to turn won tricks face-down for an additional challenge.

You may end a deal early if you can be sure that nothing will change from the rest of the deal. For example, perhaps the only thing that matters from this deal is whether you succeed on a certain task or not, and you can guarantee you will never fail this task any more; then you may end the deal early. However, figuring out "nothing will change" might be tricky; if you're unsure, just play out the deal.

About the pool

The pool is an additional communal hand. After seeding the pool, the pool should have 9 cards. All players have access to the pool, but playing from the pool requires pool tokens and is governed by some special rules. Read these rules carefully.

3-player game

In 3p, you play from the pool in addition to from your hand.

Before you play a card from your hand, you may first play a card from the pool. (Thus you play two cards this trick.) Put one of your pool tokens on top of the card from the pool. If you have no pool token remaining, you cannot play from the pool.

Note: You may only play from the pool before you play from your hand. If you are the lead player and you play from the pool, the lead card must be the one played from the pool; it cannot be the one played from your hand.

4-player game

In 4p, you play from the pool instead of from your hand.

When it's your turn to play a card, instead of playing from your hand, you may play a card from the pool. Put one of your pool tokens on top of the card. If you have no pool token remaining, you cannot play from the pool.

Note: Even though you must follow suit if able, this is only determined once you choose whether to play from your hand or from the pool. For example, even if you can follow suit from your hand and not from the pool, you may choose to play from the pool. (Then since you cannot follow suit from the pool, you may play any card from the pool.)

Regardless of player count, there is one rule that governs playing from the pool:

in each trick, exactly one player must play from the pool.

This implies the following:

  • If someone has already played from the pool this trick, then you may not play from the pool.
  • If nobody has played from the pool this trick yet, and you are the last player with any pool token remaining (usually because you are the last player of the trick), then you must play from the pool.

If you follow the above two implications (in particular the second one), you should never get into a position where it's impossible to follow the rule. If you don't play from the pool when you should have, you should undo your turn and retry it. (The other players should help catch onto this.)

It is possible that the entire group is inattentive, so that you botched up the rule and nobody else caught onto it. A mistake like this will usually become clear later in the deal, when your card counts are wrong. In this case, you have to declare the deal void.

Miscellaneous rules

The following rules govern communication:

  • Sharing private information (e.g. what cards are in your hand) goes against the spirit of the game and is strictly prohibited. Similarly, talking about anything that you can only conclude from private information (e.g. "I can win the trick if you lead hearts") is also prohibited.
  • Talking about strategy during a deal is less clear-cut, and your playgroup may decide how you want to play. I typically allow small reminders, e.g. "I still need to win a trick for the task".
  • Talking or reminding public information, such as the number of cards in your hand, or who seeded what card in the pool, is allowed. Your group may decide otherwise.
  • Outside a deal, you may always communicate freely unless otherwise stated. For example, if you need to play several related deals in sequence; you may talk between deals to adjust the expectations for future deals.
  • Strictly speaking, it is possible to devise artificial conventions, similar to that of contract bridge or Hanabi. Devising conventions to apply broadly is discouraged, and is unlikely to be worth the effort anyway. However, some conventions to tackle particularly difficult challenges are acceptable; they are called strategy.

Solo variant

The solo variant changes the game: instead of a game of limited communication, it is now a puzzle of ordering and planning.

The solo variant follows the 4p mode with some changes. You are joined with 4 bots, each bot handling one hand. Apply the following rule changes:

  • During preparation: After dealing each hand, flip each hand face-up, but do not change the order of the cards. The order of cards in the hand must be clear, from first to last. (You may splay each hand so you can see all the cards.)
  • When playing a card: If a bot plays a card from its hand, it will play the first legal card in its hand. If the bot can follow suit, it plays the first card of that suit in its hand. If the bot cannot follow suit (or if it leads), it simply plays the first card in its hand.

All other decisions are controlled by you. In particular, these include:

  • Which bot will be the captain
  • Which cards are seeded from each hand
    • You may choose any two cards from each hand. The rule about first legal card only applies when playing a card, not while seeding.
  • Whether a bot uses a pool token, and if so, which card is played from the pool
    • This is important. This decision is the primary way for you to change how the bots play.
    • You may choose any card from the pool. The rule about first legal card only applies when playing a card from a bot's hand.
  • Any other choices called for by the campaign

Whenever the rules or campaign refer to a "player", it means a bot.

Some parts of the campaign are not available in solo. (These usually involve a player making a secret choice.)

Although the above rules state the solo variant is played with the 4p mode, it is actually fully functional with the 3p mode as well. (The main reason 4p is the default is because it is slightly easier for solo.) If you want, you may choose to play using the 3p mode rules instead. This means having 3 bots instead of 4, and following the 3p rules and campaign.

Why this game was developed

Click to read

In 2019, a game called The Crew: The Quest for Planet Nine. I don't remember when I was introduced to this game, but when I picked it up, I was impressed. Using the classic trick-taking formula but implementing it in a fully cooperative game was awesome. (Contract bridge had partnerships, but it was still competitive 2v2.) I particularly loved the variety of missions, and I loved how it encouraged people to design their own custom missions.

Later on, The Crew: Mission Deep Sea and The Crew: Journey to the Ends of the Earth were released. They used the same cooperative trick-taking base, but put it in different contexts, with even more variety in mission formats. In addition, two games with mouthful titles, The Lords of the Rings: The Fellowship of the Ring — Trick-Taking Game and its sequel, also took the cooperative trick-taking base but implemented the win conditions differently. The Crew: Family Adventure was a spin-off, based on following suit/number (as in Crazy Eights and UNO), but otherwise was still cooperative with a variety of tasks.

I loved the creativity you could express through the missions. In particular, I loved the missions that did not involve task cards; I think those were the most fascinating and novel. When Ends of the Earth introduced tasks that had variable scoring, I was also enamored with them. Ends of the Earth also brought the possibility of a mission spanning multiple deals, something that I haven't considered before.

At first, I set out to develop a variant for The Crew. The goal was to introduce more of these kinds of wacky missions; I remember one of my ideas was that you could score positive or negative points, and your goal was to balance your running score so it wasn't too high or too low.

But then I realized, I didn't have to be bound to the structure of The Crew. I could develop my own game. I could put in my own unique twist that made it feel pretty different from the other cooperative trick-taking games.

I focused on making it playable using a standard deck of cards, but still keeping it intuitive. This led to the unusual stripped deck; the honors are the trump suit and vice versa, so it's easier to spot which cards are trumps. (Though ♦ diamonds and ♥ hearts having the same color is a bit annoying. I recommend playing with a four-color deck.) I experimented with a few mechanics involving "stashing" a card out of your hand, and it led to the pool mechanic; see another design note below for a more thorough explanation.

Right from the outset, I always wanted my game to be very extensible. Using the same core basic rules, you could set a variety of different goals. However, I learned this was not a good way to present your game. It took many iterations to figure out how to best write the various scenarios. At the time of this writing, it's a campaign with several worlds, each world being mostly its own thing; we'll see if that sticks.

The name "Togetrick" comes from "together" and "trick". The name is coincidentally very similar to Togetic, a Pokémon species known for happiness and joy. However, the game has no theme whatsoever, since I'm horrible at coming up with themes.

How I arrived at the pool mechanic

Click to read

The initial design of Togetrick had aimed to be extremely flexible. This included player count: although it was designed primarily for 4 players (the 3p mode didn't come until way later), I included rules to add a 5th player. This 5th player, called "ghost" at the time, wasn't a player for the game purposes. However, in each trick, exactly one player must pass from playing a card from their hand, and the ghost played a card on their behalf instead. This is already very close to the pool mechanic!

I don't know exactly how the ghost mechanic came to mind. I believe I once read an interesting rule for trick-taking games somewhere, where one player could pass from playing a card. In order to make sure the trick had exactly 4 cards, I substituted it with the ghost playing a card on their behalf instead.

At the time, each player had an individual "reserve" card. This was how players communicated back then; they revealed their reserve card face-up, and it was not counted as part of their hand for the purpose of following suit. There were additional rules about how players could exchange cards; basically, one card was reserved in the middle of the table, and players could either take it as their reserve (and put another card from their hand as the central reserve), or simply reserve one of the cards from their hand. This was bogging down the gameplay, and I struck the individual reserve idea; instead, all reserve cards were pooled together.

Finally, I realized playing a card from the common reserve was exactly how the ghost worked. I merged the two rules, and it ended up being the pool mechanic.

How I came up with the solo variant

Click to read

The initial design of Togetrick had aimed to be extremely flexible. This included player count: although it was designed primarily for 4 players (the 3p mode didn't come until way later), I included rules to subtract player counts by substituting them with artificial players.

At the time, I had two kinds of artificial players. One was a "puppet". A puppet only revealed a few (I think 4) cards from its hand, but the group may play any face-up card from the puppet. This was directly lifted from the solo rules for LOTR Trick-Taking Game.

The other was a "bot". This was inspired by a fan-made solo variant for The Crew, called Solo Running, invented by Tom Scutt. In this variant, you play a standard 4p game, but you only directly control one hand. The other three hands are controlled by bots with different behaviors: playing the first legal card, playing the highest legal card, and playing the lowest legal card. For me, the "first legal card" bot was the most fascinating, and I directly stole it for the bot. You could replace a player with the bot, and while the group could see its cards, it would play the first legal card each time, so it wasn't easy to get it to play the right card at the right time.

In the original design for Togetrick, there was no specific solo variant; solo simply meant all other hands were controlled by bots. This was very difficult; you had so little control over anything. However, I found out that combining this with the ghost player (see above) gave you just enough control to be able to plan things.

When I moved to the pool mechanic, I also dropped the artificial players; I wanted the core game to work good enough before I wanted to support anything else. However, as a primarily-solo player, I wanted to keep the solo variant. I loved how the "everyone are bots except you control the pool" rule felt like, so I put it in again.

As an aside, I knew the solo variant would never replicate the feel of the real game (with multiple players). The real game relies on limited communication and hidden information, something that is impossible solo. So I instead went for a planning puzzle: you have full information, the goal is to execute it. This is somewhat reminiscent of Space Alert, a real-time cooperative game; the full game with multiple players relies on the chaotic communication, but the solo game gives you full agency, and the challenge becomes to keep everything straight and sorted out in your head alone.
